*"Can we use this app?" is a four-word question with a surprisingly long answer.*

It happens constantly. A teacher comes to you excited about a new app they discovered at a conference, saw on a Facebook group, or got pitched by a vendor at lunch. It looks great. The kids would love it. And they want to start using it Monday.

Your job at that moment isn't to say no. It's to slow down and ask the right questions — because in K-12 education, the apps teachers use with students aren't just productivity tools. They're data collectors. And the data they're collecting belongs to children.

---

## Why This Matters More Than Most Teachers Realize

Teachers aren't trying to put student data at risk. They're trying to do their jobs better, and the edtech market has flooded them with tools that look helpful and are often free. Free, in edtech, is rarely actually free — the business model is frequently built on data.

FERPA (the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act) governs how student education records can be shared and used. COPPA (the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act) adds additional protections for students under 13. Many states have layered their own student privacy laws on top of those. Violating any of them isn't just a compliance headache — it can expose your school to real legal liability and erode the trust families place in you.

The problem is that most teachers have never read a privacy policy, and they shouldn't have to. That's what you're there for.

---

## Build an App Approval Process — Before You Need It

The worst time to figure out your third-party app policy is when a teacher is already mid-unit using an unapproved tool with 30 students' data sitting on a vendor's server somewhere.

Set up a simple, low-friction approval process and communicate it clearly to staff. It doesn't have to be complicated:

A Google Form works fine. Ask for the app name, the vendor website, how the teacher plans to use it, what grade level, and whether students will create accounts. That's enough to start your review.

Make sure teachers know the process exists, what the turnaround time is, and — critically — that the answer isn't always no. A fast, fair process that teachers trust is infinitely better than a policy they work around.

---

## What to Look For When You're Evaluating an App

When a request comes in, here's what you're actually trying to answer:

**Does the vendor have a signed data privacy agreement (DPA)?** Many states require one before student data can be shared with a third party. Common Sense Media's Privacy Program and Student Data Privacy Consortium (SDPC) maintain searchable databases of vendors who have already signed standardized agreements — start there before reaching out to vendors directly.

**What data does the app actually collect?** Read the privacy policy with a skeptical eye. Look for what's collected, how long it's retained, whether it's sold or shared with third parties, and what happens to the data when you stop using the service. Vague language like "we may share data with partners" is a red flag.

**Do students need to create accounts?** Account creation almost always means data collection. If the tool can be used in a teacher-facing or anonymous mode, that's significantly lower risk.

**Is the vendor COPPA compliant?** If any students using the app are under 13, this is non-negotiable. The vendor needs to either obtain verifiable parental consent or operate in a school-consent model where the school assumes responsibility under FERPA.

**What does your state's student privacy law require?** California, New York, Texas, and many other states have passed student privacy legislation that goes beyond federal law. Know what yours requires and check whether the vendor meets that bar.

## Create an Approved App List — and Maintain It

Once you've vetted an app and it passes, document it. Keep a running list of approved tools — even a simple shared Google Sheet visible to all staff — that includes the app name, approved grade levels, any usage restrictions, and when the approval was last reviewed.

This does two things: it saves you from reviewing the same app fifteen times as different teachers discover it, and it gives teachers a first stop before they submit a request at all.

Review the list annually. Vendors get acquired. Privacy policies change. An app that was acceptable two years ago may not meet the same bar today.

---

## How to Say No Without Killing Teacher Enthusiasm

This is the soft skill that the compliance frameworks don't cover, and it matters.

When you have to deny an app, explain why in plain language. "This vendor doesn't have a signed DPA and their privacy policy allows them to sell user data to advertisers" is a real reason a teacher can understand and respect. "It's not approved" is a wall that breeds resentment.

When you can, offer an alternative. If a teacher wants a quiz tool that doesn't pass review, point them toward one that does. Your job is to help them accomplish the instructional goal safely, not to protect students by blocking everything.

And when you approve something, say so enthusiastically. A quick email that says "good news — that app passed review, here's how to set it up" goes a long way toward building the kind of relationship where teachers bring things to you instead of just quietly installing them.

---

## The Bottom Line

Student data privacy isn't a bureaucratic checkbox. It's a genuine responsibility that families are trusting you with when they enroll their kids in your school. Third-party apps are one of the highest-risk surfaces for that data, and the edtech market moves faster than any policy can keep up with.

Your job isn't to be the person who says no to everything. It's to be the person who built a system that lets good tools in and keeps bad ones out — and who made it easy enough for teachers to use that they actually do.
